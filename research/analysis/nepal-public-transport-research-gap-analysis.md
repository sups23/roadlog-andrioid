### PAPER 1: Banasthali-Sundhara Urban Road
**Determining the Impact of Public Transportation on Urban Mobility**
*Birat Sharma & Padma Bahadur Shahi | IOEGC-10, 2021 | Pages 504–513*

#### Methodology Detail
| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Design** | Mixed-methods: secondary literature + primary surveys |
| **Sample** | **n=115** (public/private transport users + vehicle operators; gender-disaggregated but not analyzed) |
| **Survey instruments** | (a) Questionnaire Survey at stations/during trips; (b) On-Board Survey — riding randomly chosen PT vehicle, recording board/alight per station, delay types (fixed, operational), time between stops, total route time |
| **Time periods** | Morning peak (9:45–11:30 AM), evening peak (6:00–6:30 PM), off-peak (3:00 PM) |
| **Observation days** | Morning = 2 days, Daytime = 1 day, Evening micro = 1 day, Evening mini = 1 day, Intersections = normal working days across peak/slack |
| **Data sources** | 2 bus service providers (named? No — just "two bus service providers"), manual timing with watch, route length from map |
| **Study area** | 5 sq. km from Banasthali Chowk → Sundhara, Kathmandu; 4-lane blacktopped road; 9 bus stops; 28 micro + 14 mini buses |
| **Analysis** | Qualitative interpretation + quantitative aggregation — **NO statistical tests, no regression, no confidence intervals** |

#### Key Findings (full detail)
- Private vehicle travel time Banasthali→Sundhara: ~16 min; micro bus: ~22 min avg
- Micro bus avg speed: **19.94 km/hr**; mini bus: **12.61 km/hr**
- **Queue lengths:** Balaju = 210m (Narayan Gopal→Kalanki), max crossing time 7.34 min; Sorakhutte = 160m (Teku→Lainchaur), max crossing 7.32 min; Keshar Mall = 142m (Jamal→Lainchaur), max crossing 7.00 min
- **Bus stop spacing:** 270m to 1600m (range) — no consistent standard
- **Survey (n=115):** 43% for employment, 32% for education, 46% walk 11–15 min to stop, 36% cite no fixed schedule as #1 problem
- **Infrastructure:** Footpaths 1.0–2m on both sides but damaged, discontinuous on 50% of stretch; Kia/Chinese micros have insufficient headroom (passengers bend); bus stops occupied by vendors; pedestrian bars absent from Banasthali to Lainchaur
- **Root causes:** Demand overlap (uniform working hours), single city center convergence, on-street parking → 4-lane→single-lane, no public transport policy

#### Critical Flaws
1. **No statistics whatsoever** — all conclusions are descriptive. No regression, no t-test, no significance, no correlation, no model.
2. **Sample = 115** with 5+ subgroups — major subgroups become n<25, conclusions unreliable
3. **Only 2 bus service providers** — not representative of Kathmandu's fragmented PT landscape
4. **Single route, single city** — no external validity
5. **"Route length measured from map"** — introduced systematic error in speed calculations
6. **Manual timing with watch** — reaction time errors, no inter-enumerator reliability
7. **Gender data collected but never analyzed** — wasted data opportunity
8. **Zero comparison** — no benchmarks, no "before", no alternative routes
9. **Morning observation = only 2 days** — statistical noise drowns any signal
10. **Published Oct 2021 — no mention of COVID** despite pandemic-era travel behavior changes

#### Research Gaps Exposed
- No fare/cost analysis; no emissions/environmental data; no safety/accident stats; no accessibility for disabled; no travel demand modeling; no land-use/transport modeling; no economic evaluation of recommendations; no historical trends; no stakeholder interviews (government, planners, operators); no technology/ITS; no funding mechanisms; no implementation roadmap; no pedestrian counts; no weekend/holiday data

### PAPER 2: Sustainable Urban Mobility — Dharan
**Strategies for Sustainable Urban Mobility: A Case Dharan Sub-Metropolitan City**
*Suman Limbu & Padma Bahadur Shahi | IOEGC-8, 2020 | Pages 615–619*

#### Methodology Detail
| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Paradigm** | Post-Positivist, Case-Study, Inductive |
| **Data collection** | Primary: structured questionnaires, visual surveys, field observations in central Dharan; Secondary: Municipality, Town Planning, Transport Dept data |
| **Interviews** | Deputy Mayor of Dharan, Director of Public Bus Organization, Director of Taxi Organization, citizens |
| **Indicators** | **80 initial → 21 final** across 8 sustainable mobility dimensions (Affordability, Accessibility, Emissions, Noise, Safety, etc.) |
| **Rating** | Likert 1–5 (1 = poor performance → 5 = best performance) |
| **Analysis** | SPSS; 3-step OECD relative approach (1999): tool development, indicator grouping, data source evaluation |
| **NOTE: Sample size never stated** |

#### Key Findings
- **16%** of 80 indicators recommended for exclusion (data unavailable); **49/80** require special government permissions; **16** indicators (NOx, SOx) free on internet; **2** require paid access
- Data frequency: 50% measured yearly, 20% daily; 6 population/land-use indicators measured every 10+ years
- Data reliability: 58% at accuracy level 4–5 ("fairly accurate"), 12 indicators at grade 3 ("medium"), 9% fuzzy, 3% bad
- **Critical finding:** Raw data available at national/regional level but NOT at city level for most economic welfare indicators

#### Critical Flaws
1. **This is NOT a completed research study** — it's essentially a data availability assessment. The paper evaluates whether indicators CAN be measured, not what the actual performance IS.
2. **Sample size never stated** — we have no idea how many people were surveyed
3. **No results from the Likert scale survey** — no mean scores, no gap analysis, no significance tests reported from the actual questionnaire
4. **No strategies were proposed or evaluated** — despite "Strategies" in the title, the paper only says "planning strategies are proposed" but doesn't detail them
5. **Traffic counts mentioned but no data reported** — qualitative only
6. **Interviews mentioned but no content/insights reported**
7. **Confusing methodology:** Lists 80 then 21 indicators but doesn't clarify which survived filtering and why
8. **Confuses indicator availability with mobility performance** — the paper fails to make the distinction
9. **No triangulation** — interviews, questionnaire, and traffic data are never integrated

#### Research Gaps Exposed
- The gap between indicator AVAILABILITY and indicator USABILITY in a data-poor context is the actual contribution — but the paper doesn't explore this theoretically; no framework for data collection in Nepali secondary cities; no comparison with Kathmandu (where data availability differs vastly); no participatory alternative to quantitative indicators

### PAPER 3: Passengers' Perception — Service Quality
**Passengers' Perception towards Quality of Public Transportation Services in Kathmandu Valley**
*Samiksha Sharma & Rojee Pradhananga | IOEGC-10, 2021 | Pages 596–603*

#### Methodology Detail
| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Model** | SERVQUAL gap model with RECSA dimensions (Reliability, Extent of service, Comfort, Safety, Affordability) |
| **Formula** | SQᵢ = Σ(Pᵢⱼ − Eᵢⱼ) |
| **Attributes** | **22** under 5 dimensions |
| **Pilot** | n=35 |
| **Final sample** | **n=402** (35% face-to-face, 65% indirect via phone/online) |
| **Exclusions** | 15 removed (answered "never" to using PT) |
| **Data collection** | June–August 2021; avg 11 min/respondent; questionnaire translated to Nepali |
| **Statistics** | SPSS 26: Paired t-test (α=0.05), Kruskal-Wallis H test, Mann-Whitney U test |
| **Scales** | 5-point Likert (1="Not agree" → 5="Strongly agree") |

#### Key Findings
- **All 22 attributes show statistically significant negative gaps (p≈0.00)**
- Top gap: **Safety of women = −2.17** (exp 4.62, perc 2.45)
- Average dimension gaps: Safety **−2.09** (WORST) > Comfort −1.87 > Reliability −1.84 > Extent −1.76 > Affordability −1.45 (BEST)
- Kruskal-Wallis H = 84.356 (p=0.00) — dimensions differ significantly
- Mann-Whitney pairwise: Safety and Affordability differ from Reliability/Extent/Comfort; Reliability-Comfort-Extent do NOT differ from each other
- Top 10 prioritized attributes: Safety of women (−2.17), Peak capacity adequancy (−2.15), Drivers' safety measures (−2.11), Belongings safety (−2.10), Cleanliness (−2.01), Seating space (−2.00), Timely arrival (−2.00), Safe driving awareness (−1.97), Elderly/disabled access (−1.97), Zero waiting time (−1.94)
- No attribute from affordability in top 10

#### Critical Flaws
1. **65% of data collected INDIRECTLY** (phone, online forms) — huge self-selection and mode bias. The people you interview face-to-face on a bus have different demographics from those who fill online forms.
2. **SERVQUAL without attribute weights** (explicitly acknowledged) — all 22 attributes treated as equally important. "Safety of women" and "fare affordability" weighted identically despite catastrophic difference in policy priority.
3. **Purely qualitative** (explicitly acknowledged) — no quantitative service level benchmarks. You know the gap is −2.17 but don't know what reducing it to −1.00 would actually cost or require.
4. **Recruitment bias:** Respondents on buses and at stops are SELF-SELECTED users. Non-users, mode-switchers, and people who avoid PT entirely are excluded. This inflates the appearance of satisfaction (dissatisfied people already left).
5. **45% of surveys were online** during 2021 — digital access bias (excludes people without internet).
6. **Combined analysis for bus + microbus + tempo** — three fundamentally different services lumped together. A tempo passenger and a large bus passenger face different realities but produce one "bus" score.
7. **No route specificity** — valley-wide survey cannot distinguish route-level variation
8. **No temporal analysis** — single cross-section, no morning/evening/summer/winter stratification

#### Research Gaps Exposed
- Need to run this WITH attribute weights (Analytic Hierarchy Process or Conjoint Analysis)
- Need to survey NON-USERS and mode-switchers
- Need to separate by mode type and route type
- Need quantitative service metrics (headway compliance, on-time performance %) paired with perception data
- Need to model which gap reductions would actually induce modal shift (stated preference experiment)

### PAPER 4: Active Mobility — Historical Core Kathmandu
**Analysis of Active Mobility in the Historical core city of Kathmandu**
*Manish Maharjan & Padma B. Shahi | IOEGC-10, 2021 | Pages 558–562*

#### Methodology Detail
| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Paradigm** | Post-positivist + pragmatic; modified dualist, inductive logic |
| **Methods** | Literature review + international case studies + vehicle volume count + physical infrastructure measurement + questionnaire survey + key informant interviews + GIS analysis |
| **Traffic count** | 3 stretches, 3 weekdays + 1 holiday, peak hour |
| **Study area** | Kathmandu historical core: Newroad↔Dhokatole (E-W), Chetrapati↔Teku (N-S), population density >1000/ha |
| **3 stretches:** | (1) Ason→Indrachowk (0.30km, oldest markets, high pedestrian, limited vehicle); (2) Sano Pashupati→Shiva Parbati (0.15km, UNESCO heritage, fully pedestrianized); (3) Newroad Gate→Juddha Shumsher (0.35km, commercial hub, high vehicle flow) |
| **Survey** | Residents + visitors of core area |
| **NOTE: Sample size never stated** |

#### Key Findings
- **Newroad:** highest 2-wheelers + 4-wheelers; **Hanumandhoka (pedestrianized):** highest pedestrians
- Pedestrians occupy larger modal share overall, then 2-wheelers
- Only **31%** support complete vehicle ban; **62%** support 4-wheeler ban 9am–9pm only
- Main reason for private vehicles: convenience + time efficiency over AM/PT
- Problems for peds/cyclists: inadequate infrastructure, on-street parking, traffic disturbances, safety
- Majority want: improved infrastructure, managed parking/vendors (not vehicle bans)
- Additional suggestions: public toilets, solid waste management, street furniture
- Land-use conversion (residential→commercial) eroding traditional facades
- **Street lights "satisfactory" but not at standard intervals**

#### Critical Flaws
1. **Sample size NEVER REPORTED** — we have no idea if the questionnaire results come from 20 or 200 people
2. **No statistical tests** — zero. Just pie charts and bar graphs.
3. **Three stretches totaling 0.80 km** — not representative of the entire core area
4. **Vehicle count only for peak hour** — off-peak patterns unknown
5. **"Questionnaire circulated amongst residents and visitors"** — no sampling frame, no random selection, convenience sample
6. **Key informant interviews mentioned but content never directly quoted or analyzed** — wasted qualitative data
7. **GIS analysis mentioned but no spatial outputs shown** — the paper doesn't present any GIS maps or analysis
8. **Title says "Analysis of Active Mobility" but no active mobility metrics:** No pedestrian density/m², no walkability scores, no cycle counts, no walking speeds, no LOS calculations for sidewalks
9. **No comparison with other heritage cities** despite mentioning "international case studies" — these case studies are never actually cited in findings
10. **Land-use analysis is anecdotal** — claims residential→commercial conversion with zero spatial data, zero building records, zero change detection

#### Research Gaps Exposed
- Need actual walkability index (e.g., Global Walkability Index applied to Kathmandu core)
- Need pedestrian volume/density measurements
- Need willingness-to-walk modeling (stated preference for walking distance/time tradeoffs)
- Need comparative heritage mobility management across South Asian cities
- Need spatial analysis (GIS-based) of infrastructure deficits

### PAPER 5: Gender-Responsive Mobility — Lagankhel to Jamal
**Gender Responsive Urban Mobility Planning (A Case of Lagankhel to Jamal Stretch)**
*Prabina Pokharel & Sangeeta Singh | IOEGC Summer 2019 | Pages 483–490*

#### Methodology Detail
| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Design** | Descriptive research design; post-positivist paradigm; correlational + case study strategies |
| **Sample** | **n=52** (Principle of Saturation — interview stopped when responses repetitive) |
| **Instruments** | Framework matrix (literature-derived dimensions, parameters, measurable indicators); questionnaire (closed-ended); case study site observation |
| **Standards referenced** | Nepal Road Standards 2070; IRC 2012; Motor Vehicles and Transport Management Act 2049 |
| **PT case study** | Sajha Yatayat as benchmark (13–15 buses, 41–52 seats, 4–6 reserved for women, CCTV, separate entry/exit, summer 7am–8pm, winter 7am–7pm) |
| **12 bus stops** studied along Lagankhel→Jamal stretch |
| **Analysis** | Descriptive percentages; gender cross-tabulation; spatial walk-through |

#### Key Findings (with specific numbers)
- **Footpaths:** 65% uncomfortable, 59% unsafe; gender gap: 75% of females uncomfortable vs 52% males; 78% females unsafe vs 73% males safe; 27% want wider footpaths
- **Going out alone:** 51% overall comfortable; **69% of females NOT comfortable** vs **89% of males comfortable**
- **Street lights:** 53% say proper; but **54% of females** say NOT proper; **~40% of 105 lights** unlit/unmaintained; residential areas (Bakhundole) = no proper lighting at all
- **Cycling:** 50% never ride; 34% sometimes; 15% always; **63% cite vehicular lane as risky** (no dedicated cycle track)
- **Sajha Yatayat:** **98% feel safe** in Sajha vs **94% NOT safe in other buses**; 92% satisfied with women's seat reservation; load factor 2–2.5; 76% unaware of "Mero Sajha App"; 59% wait 11–15 min; 75% unaware of emergency helpline; only 1 female sub-driver (from initial 11)
- **Zebra crossings:** 34 total; **69% not satisfied** (placement too far, faint, too close to junctions)
- **Overhead bridges:** 9 total; >55% feel safe overall; **51% of women feel unsafe** vs 68% men safe; 44% find steps difficult; 21% say steps slippery/damaged/no light
- **Subway:** 1 (Ratnapark); >51% unsafe; **66% of females unsafe** vs 73% males safe; gates close at 7pm; 44% want better lighting; 30% want CCTV
- **Sexual harassment (non-Sajha buses):** 32% stared at; 30% none; 17% lost mobile/money; 14% verbal comments; **7% physically touched**; female victims: 40% stared at, 25% verbal, 15% touched; **>70% of victims are women aged 20–40**
- **After 7pm:** 45% can't get buses; buses overcrowded if they do; 19% say no proper lights; roads empty/scary
- **Public toilets:** None within 250m of PT stops (standard violation); 48% say toilets very dirty; 30% never use them

#### Critical Flaws
1. **n=52** — Principle of Saturation is a QUALITATIVE criterion. Using it to determine a quantitative survey sample is inappropriate. At n=52, percentages have massive margins of error (±14%).
2. **No inferential statistics** — despite a "correlational strategy" declared, zero correlations, zero chi-square tests, zero t-tests reported between gender and any variable. Everything is descriptive percentages.
3. **Survey conducted IN DAYTIME** (explicitly acknowledged) — so safety/lighting data for night conditions is pure RECALL, not verified by observation
4. **Single stretch** — cannot generalize to other Kathmandu corridors
5. **No qualitative depth** — despite "case study strategy" declaration, zero direct quotes, zero narrative accounts from 52 respondents
6. **Non-probability sampling** — findings cannot be generalized
7. **Conflates "gender" with "women"** throughout — men's mobility needs ignored; non-binary/transgender absent
8. **Framework matrix never validated** — taken from literature but never pilot-tested with experts or statistically validated for Kathmandu context
9. **No intersectional analysis** — c/e/t/c (caste, ethnicity, class) not considered
10. **No ethical approval mentioned** for research involving sexual harassment disclosure
11. **Interviews with "ward chairpersons and government bodies" mentioned in methodology — but their data never actually appear in findings section** — the entire findings section draws only from the 52-person survey + site observation

#### Research Gaps Exposed
- Need intersectional mobility study (gender × class × disability × migration status)
- Need nighttime direct observation (not daytime recall)
- Need comparative analysis across Sajha Yatayat and other operators with statistical controls
- Need participatory methods (safety audits, photovoice, travel diaries)
- Need modeling of willingness-to-pay for safety improvements
- Need to survey male and non-binary experiences with equal depth

### PAPER 6: Service Quality — S-5 Route
**Assessing the Service Quality of Public Transport Service: A Case of S-5 Route**
*Bibek Karki & Padma Bahadur Shahi | IOEGC Summer 2019 | Pages 739–744*

#### Methodology Detail
| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Design** | Post-Positivist, Case-Study, Inductive |
| **Sample** | Target n=96 (95% confidence, 10% CI); actual **n=107** |
| **Survey** | On-board interviews (inside bus, to minimize interruption); 3 working days, 9am–7pm |
| **14 indicators** | Reliability, comfort, security, cleanliness, waiting time, walking time, peak hour frequency, off-peak frequency, route change, fare, staff behavior, driver's recklessness, information, feedback |
| **Rating** | 5-pt Likert (1=worst, 5=best) → LOS categories: A(=1)→Worse, B(>1–≤2)→Poor, C(>2–≤3)→Average, D(>3–≤4)→Good, E(>4–≤5)→Excellent |
| **Route** | S-5 (Sinamangal↔New Buspark), 10.1 km, pilot route of KSUT project; replaced micro/tempo with 9m buses (55 cap: 25 seated + 30 standing) |
| **Analysis** | MS Excel + SPSS; means + frequencies; NO gap analysis, NO regression |

#### Key Findings
- **Overall service quality: 3.14 (Good, LOS D)**
- Walking time **3.96 (Good)**, Comfort **3.89 (Good)**, Security **3.80 (Good)**, Fare **3.79 (Good)**
- Route change **3.41 (Good)**, Staff behavior **3.37 (Good)**, Off-peak frequency **3.25 (Good)**
- Feedback **3.24 (Good)**, Driver's recklessness **3.19 (Good)**
- Information **2.99 (Average)**, Waiting time **2.94 (Average)**, Cleanliness **2.92 (Average)**
- **Peak hour frequency 1.99 (Poor)** — 93% felt infrequent
- **Service reliability 1.26 (Poor)** — 79% strongly disagreed on on-schedule service
- 68% use PT daily; 70% wait 6–9 min; 40% walk 5–10 min
- 75% male, 25% female; 62% aged 15–30

#### Critical Flaws
1. **Only perception measures** — explicitly acknowledged. No objective service data (actual headway, actual on-time %, actual travel times) collected to validate the perception scores.
2. **Only 3 working days** — day-of-week effect unknown
3. **Wide confidence interval (10%)** — the difference between "Good" (3.14) and "Average" (3.00) could be within margin of error
4. **No gap analysis** — unlike Paper 3, doesn't compare expectation vs perception. "Good comfort" (3.89 mean) doesn't tell you whether passengers expect worse or better.
5. **Gender bias: 75% male** — female experience underweighted
6. **Single route** — no comparison with other routes or modes
7. **Ticketing system not operational during survey** — key service quality variable unobservable
8. **Mechanical condition and environmental impact excluded** — stated as limitations but actually very material

#### Research Gaps Exposed
- Need combined objective+subjective evaluation on same route
- Need comparison across multiple routes to identify route-specific vs systemic issues
- Need before/after evaluation of KSUT project interventions
- Need to collect actual headway adherence and on-time performance data

### PAPER 7: Travel Time Reliability — Airport Route
**Modelling Travel Time Reliability of Public Bus Transport: Airport to Narayan Gopal Chowk**
*Santosh Kumar Thapa & Rojee Pradhananga | IOEGC-14, 2023 | Pages 451–457*

#### Methodology Detail
| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Technique** | On-board method — trained enumerators (Civil Sub-Engineers + Civil Engineering students) with stopwatch |
| **Study route** | Airport→Narayan Gopal Chowk, 5.6 km, 8 bus stops, 4 unsignalized + 1 signalized intersections |
| **Providers** | 4: Mahanagar Yatayat, Mahasagar, Mayur, Khwopa |
| **Data period** | 8:30–11:30 AM, 6 days, morning peak only |
| **Sample** | >290 total → 41 data sets (7 samples per 30-min interval per operator) |
| **Reliability measure** | 95th percentile travel time (FHWA standard) → Planning Time = MTT + Buffer Time |
| **Model** | Multiple linear regression (stepwise), SPSS; 8 independent variables + MTT |
| **Correlation pre-check** | Max inter-variable r = 0.340 (<0.7) → no multicollinearity |

#### Key Findings
- **Mean delays:** D_ucs (undefined curb stop) = **2.182 min** (LARGEST), D_sf (side friction) = **1.877 min**, D_i (intersection) = **1.198 min**, D_bs (bus stop excess) = **0.648 min**, D_ar (access road) = **0.646 min**, D_pc (pedestrian crossing) = **0.208 min**, D_ut (u-turning) = **0.182 min** (SMALLEST)
- **D_ucs and D_pc found INSIGNIFICANT** despite D_ucs having the HIGHEST mean delay — removed from final model
- **Final model:** R² = **0.775**, Adjusted R² = **0.735** (77.5% of PT variation explained)
- **Final equation:** PT = 0.817×MTT + **2.099×D_bs** + **2.613×D_i** + **3.012×D_ar** + **6.818×D_ut** + **1.780×D_sf**
- Most significant: MTT (p=0.000), D_i (p=0.000), D_sf (p=0.002)
- Highest coefficient: D_ut = **6.818** (each minute of u-turn delay → 6.818 extra min of PT)
- D_ut has lowest significance among retained vars (p=0.047)

#### Critical Flaws
1. **D_ucs has HIGHEST mean delay (2.182 min) BUT WAS FOUND INSIGNIFICANT** — this is a RED FLAG. The paper never explains this contradiction. The most common delay type on the route doesn't contribute to planning time?? This likely indicates a variance/correlation issue not properly investigated.
2. **Morning peak only** — no off-peak, no evening peak. Delay composition likely completely different at other times. Model has zero temporal transferability.
3. **41 data sets from >290 samples** — aggregation obscures trip-level variation. The effective sample is 41, not 290. With 6 retained independent variables, that's 6.8 observations per variable — dangerously underpowered.
4. **Single route** — no spatial transferability
5. **No model validation** — no train/test split, no cross-validation, no holdout data. R² = 0.775 with n=41, k=6 is likely optimistic/overfitted.
6. **Linear assumption never tested** — no residual diagnostics, no heteroscedasticity tests, no normality checks reported
7. **No passenger-facing reliability metrics** — buffer index, planning time index, misery index not calculated despite being industry standard
8. **Stepwise regression with n=41** — known to produce biased coefficients and inflated R² in small samples
9. **Expected delay at signalized intersection treated as constant** — but signal timing variation across the day is REAL; treating it as "expected" and excluding it is questionable
10. **Bus provider not used as control variable** — differences across Mahanagar/Mahasagar/Mayur/Khwopa not modeled

#### Research Gaps Exposed
- Need full-day reliability modeling (AM peak, PM peak, off-peak combined in one model)
- Need passenger-level reliability perception vs objective reliability comparison
- Need the INSIGNIFICANT paradox explained (high-mean delays dropping out of models)
- Need non-linear/machine learning alternatives compared against linear regression
- Need model validation on separate route

### PAPER 8: Efficiency & Satisfaction — Inner Ring Road
**Study on Efficiency and Satisfaction level of Public Transportation within Kathmandu Inner Ring Road**
*Bishnu Bhattarai | MSc Thesis, TU Pulchowk Campus, February 2017*

#### Methodology Detail
| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Efficiency method** | Moving Observer (Floating Car); **DEA (BCC/VRS output-oriented)**; software: DEAP 2.1 |
| **DEA inputs (3)** | Travel time per trip (min), Waiting time at station (min), Running speed (m/s) |
| **DEA output (1)** | Average passenger in/out per trip |
| **Bus data** | **30 datasets** from 5 providers (15 clockwise, 15 anti-clockwise), across AM/PM peak + off-peak |
| **Micro-bus data** | **18 datasets** from 3 providers (9 clockwise, 9 anti-clockwise) |
| **Satisfaction method** | Revealed-preference questionnaire; **n=384** (95% CI, infinite pop); SPSS paired t-test (α=0.05) |
| **10 satisfaction indicators** | 5-pt Likert (1=high, 5=low); benchmark mean=2.5 |
| **Model calibration** | Regression: DEA efficiency = f(travel time, waiting time, running speed, avg passenger) |
| **Study area** | Inner Ring Road (H016), 27.3 km, encircling CBD; >1 lakh passengers/day (Hemant 2015) |

#### Key Findings

**DEA Efficiency:**
- Buses **24.5% more efficient** than micro-buses overall
- Mahanagar Yatayat = 100% (benchmark); Other buses avg = **86.6%**; Micro-buses avg = **56.5%**
- Bus operator rankings: Mahanagar 100% > Lalitpur 96.8% > Swayambhu 90.3% > City 87.4% > Nepal Yatayat 83.2% = Karya Binayak Mount Everest 83.2%
- Micro-bus rankings: Manakamana 100% = Koteshwor 100% > Mahalaxmi 97.2%

**Field Data:**
- Bus avg: Travel time 173.96 min, Waiting 45.69 min, Passengers in 112.22, out 114.93, Journey speed 9.792 km/h
- Micro avg: Travel time 181.33 min, Waiting 53.07 min, Passengers in 60.94, out 58.83, Journey speed 9.216 km/h
- Mahanagar: Travel time 121.00 min, Waiting 37.50 min, Passengers 137 each, Journey speed 14.22 km/h

**Regression:** Y = 6.04 − 0.00105×Travel Time + 0.48×Waiting Time − 0.323×Running Speed + 0.56×Passenger (R² = **0.97**)

**Satisfaction (all means >2.5 = UNSATISFACTORY for BOTH modes):**
- Bus means: Comfort 2.88, Cleanliness 3.26, Frequency 3.18, Disabled access 3.21, Timetable info 3.39, Seating 3.35, Overcrowding 3.51, Speed 3.26, Conductor 3.38, Boarding ease 3.39
- Bus SIGNIFICANTLY better than micro on 6/10 indicators (p<0.05): comfort, disabled access, seating, overcrowding, conductor, boarding ease
- No significant difference on 4: cleanliness (p=0.69), frequency (p=0.16), timetable (p=0.11), speed (p=0.26)
- **NO indicator favors micro-bus** in any statistically significant way
- Discouraging factors: Crowding 40%, Delay 33%, Poor treatment 14%, No seat 13%
- **75% wait >5 min**; 31% wait >15 min

#### Critical Flaws
1. **DEA with 3 inputs + 1 output: overly simple.** Standard transit DEA uses 5–10 inputs (cost, fleet size, labor, fuel, capacity) + 4–8 outputs (vehicle-km, seat-km, passenger-km, revenue). The thesis explicitly acknowledges "physical characteristics of vehicles and lane width NOT incorporated."
2. **Scale efficiency vs technical efficiency confusion.** Bus and micro-bus both have VRS technical efficiency = 1.000. The 24.5% difference comes from SCALE efficiency (bus=1.000, micro=0.755). The thesis never clearly explains this. The claim "bus is 24.5% more efficient" conflates scale efficiency with overall efficiency.
3. **Tiny regression sample.** The Mahanagar-Bus-Micro regression (n=3, df=1) yields R²=0.987 but p=0.072 — clearly overfitted and meaningless. Yet it's presented as a finding.
4. **Bus operators regression (n=6) borderline significant** (p=0.0518, R²=0.653). With n=6 and 4 predictors, this should not be treated as reliable.
5. **The satisfaction model is purely descriptive** — only paired t-tests. Literature review discusses Binary Logistic Regression at length (Section 2.7, pages 24–25, 7 paragraphs) but **it is never used**. The thesis reviews a method it doesn't apply.
6. **Mahanagar Yatayat satisfaction NEVER SURVEYED.** The benchmark operator for efficiency has zero satisfaction data. We can't compare the most efficient operator's passenger experience.
7. **Perception data collected but demographics not analyzed.** Age, gender, occupation data exists but the thesis reports zero satisfaction segmentation — all 384 responses lumped together.
8. **Clockwise vs anti-clockwise direction collected but never analyzed as a variable** — missed opportunity for directional bias analysis
9. **No inter-item reliability** (Cronbach's alpha not reported) — we don't know if the 10 satisfaction indicators form a coherent scale
10. **Moving observer method on a 27.3 km ring road with 1 observer** — the observer records everything for their vehicle, but PT behavior at one end of the ring affects conditions at the other end; single-observer method introduces temporal autocorrelation

#### Research Gaps Exposed
- Need multi-input/multi-output DEA with cost and capacity variables
- Need to survey Mahanagar Yatayat passengers AND concurrently survey PT non-users
- Need satisfaction modeling (not just description) using ordered logit/probit
- Need directional analysis (clockwise vs anti-clockwise efficiency/satisfaction)
- Need demographic segmentation of satisfaction
- Need longitudinal efficiency analysis (pre/post KSUTP intervention)

## PART 2: CROSS-CUTTING RESEARCH GAPS

### GAP 1: Methodological Monoculture
| Dimension | Across ALL 8 Papers |
|-----------|---------------------|
| **Paradigm** | 7/8 explicitly "Post-Positivist" (one pragmatic). NOT ONE interpretive, critical, participatory, or feminist paradigm — despite Paper 5 on gender using post-positivist. |
| **Method** | 8/8 use questionnaire surveys with Likert scales. 0/8 use travel diaries, GPS tracking, big data, mobile phone data, or ethnographic methods. |
| **Statistics** | Only Papers 3, 7, and 8 use ANY inferential statistics. Papers 1, 2, 4, 5, 6 are purely descriptive. |
| **Modeling** | Only Papers 7, 8 have predictive models. Papers 1–6 are descriptive with zero forecasting power. |
| **Sampling** | All convenience or poorly defined. Sample sizes range from 52 (satisficed!) to 402 but with massive subgroup issues. |

### GAP 2: The Non-User Blind Spot
**Every single paper surveys CURRENT PT USERS ONLY.** No paper surveys:
- People who switched from PT to private vehicles (and WHY)
- People who NEVER use PT at all
- Tourists (relevant for Papers 1, 4, 5)
- Disabled people as a targeted sample
- Night shift workers
- Informal workers (street vendors, construction workers whose mobility patterns differ)
- Children and elderly as separate respondent groups
- Caregivers whose trips are trip-chained (school drop → work → shop → home)

**This is selection bias on a massive scale.** The people most dissatisfied with PT have already left. Surveying only remaining users inflates satisfaction scores and hides the most critical problems.

### GAP 3: No Temporal Dimension
- **All papers are cross-sectional.** Not one longitudinal study.
- No pre/post intervention analysis (e.g., did Sajha Yatayat improve anything? KSUTP S-5 route before/after?)
- No seasonal variation (monsoon vs dry season — footpath conditions, passenger load, delays)
- No weekend vs weekday analysis except Paper 4's 1 holiday
- **No time-series data on key variables** — is PT getting better or worse over years?

### GAP 4: Environmental Variables Completely Absent
- **Zero papers measure actual air quality, noise, or emissions** despite ALL discussing "pollution" as a problem
- Paper 2 lists "Air polluting emissions, Noise hindrance, GHG emissions" as INDICATORS but never measures them

- **No paper links PT service quality to environmental outcomes**

### GAP 5: Economic Analysis Absent

- No fare elasticity modeling
- No analysis of PT as a % of household expenditure by income group
- No valuation of travel time savings
- No willingness-to-pay for specific service improvements
- No financial sustainability analysis of operator models

### GAP 6: Technology Blindness

## PART 3: CRITICAL ERRORS & METHODOLOGICAL ISSUES ACROSS PAPERS

### Error 1: Confidence Intervals Treat PT Population as Infinite
**Papers 3, 6, 8** treat the public transport user population as "infinite" to calculate sample sizes. This is wrong. The PT-using population of Kathmandu is finite and well-bounded. Treating it as infinite inflates required sample sizes (wasting effort) or — paradoxically — gives a false sense of precision with the wrong formula.

### Error 2: Declared Paradigms Don't Match Methods
- **Paper 5** declares "post-positivist paradigm" for a gender study. Feminist epistemology explicitly critiques post-positivism. A feminist/gender-responsive study using post-positivist methods is a philosophical contradiction.
- **Paper 4** declares "pragmatic paradigm, modified dualist, inductive logic" — this is a confusion of terms. Pragmatism, dualism, and inductivism are from different philosophical traditions and are not coherently integrated.

### Error 3: Likert Scales Treated as Interval Data Without Testing
Papers 2, 3, 6, 8 compute means, run t-tests, and do linear regression on Likert scale data. Likert data is ORDINAL, not interval. Treating it as interval for parametric statistics requires justification (e.g., demonstrating approximate normality, using 5+ categories). Only Paper 3 even mentions this implicitly (by using non-parametric Mann-Whitney U alongside t-tests).

### Error 4: DEA Observations < 3× Inputs
**Paper 8's DEA** for bus operators uses n=6 DMUs with 3 inputs. The rule of thumb is n ≥ max(m×s, 3(m+s)) where m=inputs, s=outputs → n ≥ 3(3+1)=12. With n=6, the DEA overestimates efficiency and produces meaningless discrimination.

### Error 5: Qualitative + Quantitative Not Integrated
Papers 1, 2, 4, 5 declare mixed methods but:
- Survey results and interview/key informant data are presented in SEPARATE sections with no integration
- Qualitative data enriches nothing — it's decoration, not analysis
- Triangulation is CLAIMED but never demonstrated

### Error 6: "Hypothesis Testing" on Convenience Samples
Papers 3 and 8 report formal hypothesis tests (t-tests, H-test, U-test) with p-values. But these tests assume probability sampling. All surveys use convenience/non-probability sampling. The p-values are mathematically correct but logically meaningless — they tell you the probability of the observed difference in YOUR SAMPLE, which cannot be generalized to the population.

### Error 7: Sample Size vs. Power Analysis
**Not one paper reports a power analysis.** Adequate sample size is determined by a priori power analysis (effect size × significance level × power). Instead:
- Paper 1: n=115 (arbitrary)
- Paper 2: unknown
- Paper 3: n=402 (based on "literature on service quality uses 150–500")
- Paper 4: unknown
- Paper 5: n=52 ("Principle of Saturation" — qualitative criterion misapplied)
- Paper 6: n=107 (calculated with 10% margin of error — which is enormous)
- Paper 7: n=41 datasets (following Green's rule but with 8 predictors it's underpowered)
- Paper 8: n=384 (for satisfaction) and n=6–15 (for DEA)

## PART 4: NOVEL RESEARCH IDEAS FROM IDENTIFIED GAPS

### IDEA 1: "The Churners" — Mode-Switcher Decision Modeling
**Gaps addressed:** Non-user blind spot (Gap 2), No temporal dimension (Gap 3)

**Why new:** Every paper studies only current PT users. The most important population — those who LEFT PT for private vehicles — has never been studied in Nepal.

**Research question:** "What service quality thresholds trigger modal shift from public to private transport in Kathmandu Valley, and what would it take to reverse the shift?"

**Proposed approach:**
- Survey 300 former PT users who now use motorcycles/cars (recruited at workplaces, parking lots, fuel stations)
- Stated preference discrete choice experiment: respondents choose between hypothetical PT scenarios (varying reliability, safety, comfort, fare, frequency)
- Estimate random parameters logit or mixed logit model to derive willingness-to-pay for each service attribute
- Identify the "tipping point" attribute improvements that would induce shift BACK to PT

**Novel contribution:** The first mode choice model for Nepal that uses people who ALREADY SHIFTED to estimate re-shift potential. This directly answers the policy question nobody has answered: how much do we need to improve PT to actually reduce private vehicle ownership?

---

### IDEA 2: "Reliability Perception Gap" — Subjective vs Objective Travel Time Reliability
**Gaps addressed:** Methods without validation (Papers 6, 7), Temporal dimension (Gap 3)

**Why new:** Paper 7 models objective travel time reliability. Paper 3 measures subjective satisfaction with reliability. Nobody has compared the two. Do passengers' perceptions of reliability match actual reliability, and what drives misperception?

**Research question:** "What is the relationship between objectively measured bus travel time reliability and passenger-perceived reliability on the S-5 route, and what factors explain the perception gap?"

**Proposed approach:**
- Simultaneously collect: (a) GPS-tracked travel times on S-5 route for 20 working days (AM, PM, off-peak) — calculate buffer index, PTI, on-time %; (b) survey passengers on SAME days about their perception of reliability
- Model the gap: Perception_score − Objective_reliability_index = f(demographics, trip purpose, waiting experience, information availability)
- Use generalized estimating equations or multilevel models

**Novel contribution:** First study in Nepal to link objective transport engineering data with subjective passenger perception data for the same trips. Will reveal whether poor reliability perception is driven by actual unreliability or by information failures (lack of schedules, apps, real-time info).

---

### IDEA 3: "The Dharan Gap" — Comparative Urban Mobility in Secondary Nepali Cities
**Gaps addressed:** Spatial coverage bias (Gap 8), Methods monoculture (Gap 1)

**Why new:** 7/8 papers study Kathmandu Valley. Dharan (Paper 2) is essentially a data availability study. Pokhara, Biratnagar, Nepalgunj, Butwal, Bhairahawa, Janakpur — zero studies. These cities are growing faster than Kathmandu and have COMPLETELY different PT systems.

**Research question:** "How do public transport systems, user demographics, and service quality perceptions differ across Nepal's three tiers of cities (metropolitan, sub-metropolitan, municipality), and what urban form factors explain the differences?"

**Proposed approach:**
- Comparative case study across 6 cities: Kathmandu (metro), Pokhara + Biratnagar (sub-metro), Nepalgunj + Butwal + Dharan (municipality)
- Replicate Paper 3's SERVQUAL approach with standardized methodology across all 6 cities
- Add urban form variables: population density, road density, land-use mix, city shape, PT route network topology
- Use multi-level structural equation modeling (ML-SEM): Level 1 = individual passengers, Level 2 = route characteristics, Level 3 = city characteristics
- Study BOTH formal and informal PT (rickshaws, tempos, shared jeeps)

**Novel contribution:** First cross-city transport comparison in Nepal. Will identify whether Kathmandu's problems are uniquely Kathmandu problems or generic urbanization problems. Will also test whether Paper 2's indicator framework can actually be operationalized.

---

### IDEA 4: "Build It and They Will Walk?" — Before/After Active Mobility Infrastructure Evaluation
**Gaps addressed:** No temporal dimension (Gap 3), Paper 4's missing metrics (Gap 4)

**Why new:** Paper 4 studies active mobility but has no before/after, no walkability index, and no pedestrian count data. Kathmandu is currently pedestrianizing Durbar Square area and building footpath improvements — perfect natural experiment.

**Research question:** "What is the causal effect of pedestrianization and footpath infrastructure improvements on pedestrian volumes, walking speeds, retail activity, and air quality exposure in the Kathmandu historical core?"

**Proposed approach:**
- Identify segments scheduled for pedestrianization or footpath improvement (quasi-experimental design)
- Pre-intervention data collection: pedestrian counts (manual + camera), pedestrian LOS, walkability audit (GWI), air quality (PM2.5/PM10 + CO at pedestrian breathing height), retail footfall survey of shopkeepers
- Post-intervention (3-month, 6-month, 12-month): repeat all measures
- Difference-in-differences analysis with control streets (no improvement planned)
- Also: intercept surveys before and after to detect mode shift

**Novel contribution:** First causal evidence on whether pedestrian infrastructure investment in Nepal changes behavior, not just opinions. Connects active mobility to public health (air quality exposure) and economic outcomes (retail activity).

---

### IDEA 5: "Two Cities, One Border" — Cross-Border Public Transport Integration (Nepal-India)
**Gaps addressed:** Spatial coverage (Gap 8), Intermodal integration (Gap 9), Non-user studies (Gap 2)

**Why new:** Zero papers study cross-border mobility despite massive flows: Nepal-India border crossings (Kakarbhitta, Birgunj, Bhairahawa, Nepalgunj, Mahendranagar) see thousands of daily passenger movements with no formal PT integration.

**Research question:** "What are the barriers to integrated cross-border public transportation between Nepal and India, and what institutional framework would enable seamless cross-border PT?"

**Proposed approach:**
- Multi-site case study: 3 major border crossings (Birgunj-Raxaul, Bhairahawa-Sunauli, Kakarbhitta-Panitanki)
- Methods: passenger intercept surveys + bus operator interviews + border official interviews + observational time-motion study of border crossing process
- Map the COMPLETE passenger journey (door-to-door) including mode changes, wait times, document checks, informal payments
- Compare with best-practice cross-border integration (EU, East Africa)
- Propose institutional mechanism (bilateral transport agreement, mutual recognition of permits, integrated ticketing)

**Novel contribution:** Opens an entirely new sub-field of Nepali transport research. Cross-border mobility is massive, unstudied, and has direct implications for trade, labor migration, and regional integration.

---

### IDEA 6: "Cost of Safety" — Economic Valuation of Women's Security in Kathmandu Public Transport
**Gaps addressed:** Economic analysis absent (Gap 5), Safety never quantified (Gap 10), Gender study flaws (Paper 5)

**Why new:** Paper 5 documents what women face but never asks what they'd PAY to avoid it. Paper 3 shows safety is the #1 gap but never attaches a rupee value to closing it.

**Research question:** "What is women's willingness-to-pay for safety improvements on Kathmandu public transport, and what is the aggregate economic cost of women's mobility constraints due to safety concerns?"

**Proposed approach:**
- Contingent valuation survey of 500 women (stratified by age, income, occupation, regular PT route)
- WTP for specific safety features: CCTV, women-only sections, emergency buttons, well-lit stops, female conductors/drivers, late-night service with security
- Trip avoidance cost: estimate how many trips women AVOID due to safety concerns → value lost trips at wage rate + opportunity cost
- Calculate aggregate annual cost of gendered mobility constraint for Kathmandu Valley

**Novel contribution:** Translates gender safety from a social issue into an economic argument. This is the kind of data that changes policy — when you can say "women's mobility constraints cost the Kathmandu economy X million NPR/year."
